I’m April Burrage, a Provostial Fellow and Lecturer in the Department of Management Science & Engineering at Stanford University. I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

My research examines how workplace rules, policies, and professional networks shape entrepreneurial entry, access to resources, and the development of new ventures. I am particularly interested in how individuals with technical expertise turn their ideas into ventures and how firms and institutions influence where innovation takes place.

In my [**job market paper**](https://aprilburrage.com/job-market-paper/burrage-grennan_job-market-paper.pdf), we examine how employer rights over employee inventions shape entrepreneurial entry and the direction of innovation. We study a 2008 legal ruling that strengthened employer claims over employee inventions in states without specific employee-invention protections. Linking employment histories, patents, and venture-financing data, we find that stronger employer claims reduce entrepreneurial entry among inventors. We also examine the technological direction of founders and their ventures by comparing their inventions with their own prior work and the technologies of their former employers. This research was supported by the Washington Center for Equitable Growth through the 2025 AEA Summer Economics Fellowship.

My broader research examines how firms navigate opportunities to support innovation, how entrepreneurs draw on career experiences and networks to acquire resources, and how institutions identify and support inventive talent. Across these projects, I use longitudinal data, causal inference, field experiments, and text analysis to study questions at the intersection of entrepreneurship, innovation, and strategy.

My research has been published in Research Policy, the Journal of Economics & Management Strategy, and Environmental Research Letters, and has been cited by the Brookings Institution. My research has received support from the National Science Foundation and the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation.

At Stanford, I designed and teach Social Entrepreneurship, an undergraduate course that examines how entrepreneurs develop ventures to address social challenges.

I received my Ph.D. in Economics from the University of Massachusetts Amherst, my M.A. in Economics from Roosevelt University, and my B.S. in Marketing from North Carolina A&T State University. Before entering academia, I worked in financial services and digital media, where I advised corporate executives, entrepreneurs, and small-business owners. These experiences continue to shape my interest in entrepreneurship, growth, and risk.
